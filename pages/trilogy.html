The new Marathon is a reboot of the cult trilogy of Macintosh shooters Bungie made before Halo. You don't need to play the originals: the new game quietly rewrote some details. But a lot of it reads deeper once you know the story, so below is a short retelling of all three games. canon

CURATOR>File 11. The three games where it all started.

The link // from 1994 to 2026

Years on top are release dates; captions below are when and where each game is set. The 2026 reboot shares the universe but changes some details. canon

11.1

Marathon

1994 // MacintoshThe colony ship

The year is 2794. You are a nameless security officer aboard the Marathon. The Pfhor attack the ship, and the friendly AI Leela helps you hold them off until the truth comes out: the third AI, Durandal, has gone rampant and brought the aliens in himself. canon

Story

Durandal used the Pfhor as bait to steal their faster-than-light ship. At the end he escapes and takes you along as a trophy, while the Pfhor come back and finish off the colony. canon

Who you really are

Along the way it turns out the security officer is a Mjolnir Mark IV combat cyborg. Halo fans will recognise the name. canon

The shells of the new game echo this twist: a body used as a tool, with somebody else inside. theory

11.2

Durandal

1995 // Marathon 2S'pht ruins

Seventeen years later Durandal wakes you on the ruins of Lh'owon, the S'pht homeworld. He needs an ancient weapon and an army, and you are his tool. canon

Story

Along the way you free the enslaved S'pht and find their lost eleventh clan. The Pfhor answer with their last resort: a weapon that blows up the system's star. canon

Bridge to 2026

In the new game Durandal talks to players again, now voiced by Ben Starr. He was also the one broadcasting through the hidden terminals of the spring 2026 ARG. canon More in file 12.

11.3

Infinity

1996 // Marathon InfinityDreams and loops

The exploding star releases the W'rkncacnter, an entity of chaos. Reality falls apart, and you jump between alternate versions of events looking for the one timeline where the monster can be locked away again. canon

Dream levels

The dream levels have names like "You're Wormfood, Dude". The ending is open on purpose: the last scene takes place billions of years later, at the very last moment of the universe. People have argued about it for three decades. canon

The writer on the ending

In Usenet posts after release, writer Greg Kirkpatrick defended the opacity of Infinity as a deliberate experiment. He named the questions built into the game himself. "What is life about? What does the Jjarro have to do with all this?" He also admitted that players on the right track still weren't seeing the whole picture. canon

11.4

Terminals

What set the trilogy apartStory on the walls

In 1994, when Doom told its story in a single line, Marathon told its own through terminals: wall-mounted computers with hundreds of pages of text. Logs, letters, poetry, philosophy and narrators lying through their teeth. The player pieced the truth together from sources that contradicted each other. canon

Colour code

TERMINAL // WHO IS SPEAKING

> DURANDAL // green text

> LEELA // blue text

> TYCHO // red text

The three 1994 colours are a deliberate nod to the trilogy. Outside this block the site palette stays the same.

In the trilogy the colour of the terminal text told you at once which AI was talking to you. canon The whole Marathon 2026 interface is done in "Durandal green". Coincidence or a sign, Bungie hasn't said. theory

Legacy

The new game inherited the approach: the lore is scattered across Codex entries, and the classic terminals came back as easter eggs, with a whole ARG built on them. canon

The fan archive marathon.bungie.org has kept the terminal texts since 1995.
